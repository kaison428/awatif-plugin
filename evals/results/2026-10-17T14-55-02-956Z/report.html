# Pinned vs. Fixed Supports

## What each support restrains

The difference comes down to whether the support stops the member from rotating.

| | **Pinned (hinged)** | **Fixed (rigid / encastré)** |
|---|---|---|
| Translation (horizontal & vertical) | Restrained | Restrained |
| Rotation | **Free** | **Restrained** |
| Reactions (2D) | 2: horizontal force Rx, vertical force Ry | 3: Rx, Ry, **moment M** |
| Reactions (3D) | 3 forces | 3 forces + 3 moments |
| Moment at the support | Zero | Non-zero, often the peak moment |
| Symbol | Triangle with a pin | Hatched wall or base |

## Effect on structural behaviour

**Pinned supports:**
- The member can rotate at the support, so the bending moment there is zero.
- Moments move into the span. A simply supported beam has M_max = wL²/8 at midspan.
- Deflections and column effective lengths are larger. A pinned-pinned column has K = 1.0. A cantilever column that is fixed at the base and free at the top has K = 2.0.
- They do not transfer moment into the foundation, so footings are smaller and simpler.
- The structure is less redundant and usually less sensitive to settlement and temperature changes.

**Fixed supports:**
- The support resists rotation and develops a moment reaction.
- For a fixed-fixed beam under a uniform load, end moments are wL²/12 and the midspan moment is wL²/24. Deflection is about 5 times smaller than for the simply supported case.
- Columns are stiffer and more stable. A fixed-fixed column has K = 0.5 in theory, and about 0.65 is used in design practice.
- The foundation must resist moment. That means bigger footings, piles or rock anchors, and soil that can resist rotation.
- The structure is more indeterminate. This adds redundancy, but differential settlement, temperature and shrinkage then cause extra stresses.

## When each is used in buildings

**Pinned supports are used for:**
- **Steel column bases in braced frames.** Base plates with 2–4 anchor bolts are usually modelled as pinned, because lateral load goes through the bracing or core.
- **Simple beam-to-column connections.** Fin plates, web cleats and shear tabs are treated as pins, the "simple construction" approach.
- **Portal frames on poor soil.** Pinned bases keep the foundations economical, at the cost of heavier rafters and columns.
- **Bearings for long-span roofs and transfer elements.** These let rotation and thermal movement happen without fighting it. Often one end is pinned and the other is a roller or slide.
- **Timber and light-gauge structures.** Their connections can't realistically develop full fixity.

**Fixed supports are used for:**
- **Moment-resisting frames without bracing.** The column bases and beam-column joints provide the lateral stability.
- **Reinforced concrete construction.** Monolithic pours of columns into footings, walls into rafts, and beams into columns naturally give near-fixed joints.
- **Cantilevers.** Balconies, canopies and overhangs need fixity at the root.
- **Shear walls and cores.** They are fixed at the foundation or raft so they can resist overturning from wind and seismic loads.
- **Tall or slender columns where drift and stability control the design,** such as flagpole-type structures, industrial sheds with cranes, and signage.
- **Rigid foundations.** These are needed for fixity to be real: large pad footings, pile caps with several piles, or footings on rock.

## Practical caveats

- **Real supports are semi-rigid.** No base is perfectly pinned or perfectly fixed. Codes such as EC3 Part 1-8 and AISC address partial fixity. A common approach is to model base stiffness with rotational springs, or to check the design under both assumptions.
- **The model must match the detail.** Assuming fixity in the analysis but detailing a flexible base plate on a small footing is unconservative for drift and stability. Assuming pins where the joint is actually rigid can overstress the connection and the foundation.
- **Soil matters.** A fixed base on soft clay can rotate, so the footing's rotational stiffness should be checked.
- **Rule of thumb:** use pinned bases where something else (bracing, cores, shear walls) provides stability. Use fixed bases where the frame itself has to resist lateral load or where a cantilever action is needed.

# Pinned vs. Fixed Supports

## The Core Difference

A support restrains movement. The two types differ in **whether they also resist rotation**.

| | **Pinned (hinged)** | **Fixed (rigid/encastré)** |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal movement | Restrained | Restrained |
| Vertical movement | Restrained | Restrained |
| Rotation | **Free** | **Restrained** |
| Reactions (2D) | 2 forces (Rx, Ry) | 2 forces + 1 moment (Rx, Ry, M) |
| Moment at support | Zero | Non-zero |
| Symbol | Triangle on a base | Hatched wall / solid block |

**Analogy:** A pinned support works like a door hinge. The member can't slide off, but it can rotate. A fixed support works like a flagpole set in concrete. It can't move or rotate.

## Structural Consequences

**Moments and deflection.** A fixed-fixed beam under uniform load has a midspan moment of wL²/24 and support moments of wL²/12. A simply supported beam has a midspan moment of wL²/8. Fixed beams deflect about **5× less** (wL⁴/384EI vs. 5wL⁴/384EI). Fixity spreads moment more evenly along the member, so members can be lighter and stiffer.

**Determinacy.** Fixed supports add reactions, which makes a structure more statically indeterminate. That gives redundancy, because loads can redistribute if something yields. It also makes the structure sensitive to settlement, temperature change, and shrinkage, all of which cause extra internal forces.

**Lateral stability.** A pinned-base portal frame sways more than a fixed-base one and needs stiffer beams or bracing to compensate.

## When Each Is Used in Buildings

### Pinned supports are used when:
- **Foundations are weak or rotation-prone.** Soft soils, or small pad footings that can't resist moment without becoming very large. Assuming fixity there is unconservative.
- **Steel portal frames** (warehouses, industrial sheds). Pinned bases keep foundations small and cheap. The frame gets its stiffness from rigid knee and apex joints.
- **Simple steel connections.** Fin plates and web cleats on beam-to-column connections are designed as pins. Stability then comes from braced frames or cores.
- **Settlement or thermal movement is expected.** Pins tolerate rotation without building up large moments.
- **Simplicity is wanted.** The structure is easier to analyse and the connections are cheaper to fabricate.

### Fixed supports are used when:
- **Lateral stiffness is needed without bracing.** Moment frames in mid-rise buildings, or cantilever columns.
- **The foundation can genuinely take moment.** Large rafts, pile groups with pile caps, or bedrock.
- **Cantilevers.** Balconies, canopies, and retaining walls must be fixed at the root. They are unstable otherwise.
- **Cast-in-place reinforced concrete.** Monolithic column-footing and beam-column joints are naturally close to fixed.
- **Seismic or high-wind regions.** Moment resistance at the base helps control drift. Pinned bases plus separate bracing or shear walls is also common there.
- **Deflection or vibration controls the design.** Long spans and sensitive floors benefit from the stiffness fixity adds.

## Practical Caveat

Real supports are almost never perfectly pinned or perfectly fixed. They are **semi-rigid**. A "fixed" base on compressible soil rotates a little, and a "pinned" bolted base plate still resists some moment. Engineers usually pick whichever assumption is conservative for the check at hand. For example, they might assume pinned bases to size beams for midspan moment, and fixed bases to check the support connections. Where accuracy matters, they model rotational springs to represent partial fixity. Design codes such as Eurocode 3, Part 1-8 give rules for classifying connections as nominally pinned, semi-rigid, or rigid.
