## Pinned vs. fixed supports

The difference comes down to which movements the support stops, and so which reactions it can supply.

### Pinned (hinged) support
- **Stops:** translation in every direction.
- **Allows:** rotation.
- **Reactions:** forces only. In 2D that's a horizontal and a vertical force (Rx, Ry). In 3D it's Rx, Ry, Rz.
- **Moment at the support:** zero.
- **Symbol:** a triangle under the member.

### Fixed (rigid or encastré) support
- **Stops:** translation and rotation.
- **Reactions:** forces plus a moment. In 2D that's Rx, Ry and Mz. In 3D it's all three forces and all three moments.
- **Moment at the support:** can be large, often the biggest moment in the column.
- **Symbol:** a member built into hatched ground.

### How they behave differently

| Aspect | Pinned base | Fixed base |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral stiffness of a portal frame | Lower | Much higher (often 2–4× for the same sections) |
| Lateral drift | Larger | Smaller |
| Column effective length (sway frame) | K ≈ 2.0 or more | K ≈ 1.0–1.2 |
| Moment sent into the foundation | None | Significant |
| Foundation size and cost | Smaller, cheaper | Larger (resists overturning), costlier |
| Sensitivity to settlement or temperature | Low | High (restraint causes extra stresses) |
| Degree of indeterminacy | Lower | Higher |

Example: a cantilever column with a lateral load P at height h has a base moment of P·h when fixed. A pinned column on its own can't be a cantilever at all. It's a mechanism unless something else braces it.

### When each is used in buildings

**Pinned bases suit:**
- **Steel portal frames** for industrial buildings and warehouses on ordinary soil. Small pad footings, simple 2–4 bolt base plates, cheaper foundations.
- **Braced frames.** Bracing or a core takes the lateral load, so columns only carry gravity and their bases can be pinned.
- **Poor or variable soil,** where a footing can't reliably stop rotation.
- **Truss supports and simply supported beams on bearings.** One end is pinned and the other is a roller so the member can expand with temperature.
- **Timber posts** and many light-gauge steel connections.

**Fixed bases suit:**
- **Moment-resisting frames** with no bracing, where column bending provides lateral stiffness for wind and earthquakes.
- **Cantilever columns,** such as canopies, single-storey sheds without bracing, signposts and light poles.
- **Cast-in-place reinforced concrete.** Monolithic column-to-footing or column-to-raft joints are naturally close to fixed.
- **Tall or crane-carrying buildings** where drift and deflection limits govern.
- **Shear walls and cores** fixed into a raft or pile cap.
- **Good ground** (rock or stiff soil) or large pile groups that can actually resist the rotation.

### Practical caveat
Real connections sit between the two ideals. A "pinned" steel base plate with four bolts has some stiffness. A "fixed" base on soft soil rotates. Designers handle this in a few ways:
- **Pick the conservative assumption for each check.** For example, design columns and drift as pinned, but check the foundation for some moment.
- **Model semi-rigid supports** with rotational springs (k = M/θ). Some codes do this, for instance EN 1993-1-8, which suggests nominal base stiffness values.
- **Make the detail match the model.** A fixed base needs a thick plate, many bolts, stiffeners and a footing sized for the moment. A pinned base should be detailed so it really can rotate.

**Rule of thumb:** use pinned bases when something else (bracing, cores) provides lateral stability or the ground is weak. Use fixed bases when the columns themselves have to resist lateral load and the foundation can supply the moment.

## Pinned vs. fixed supports

The difference is which movements each support stops, and which reactions it can therefore carry.

| | **Pinned (hinged)** | **Fixed (rigid / encastré)** |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal translation | Restrained | Restrained |
| Vertical translation | Restrained | Restrained |
| Rotation | **Free** | **Restrained** |
| Reactions (2D) | Rx, Ry (2) | Rx, Ry, **M** (3) |
| Moment at support | Zero | Non-zero (the support resists bending) |
| Symbol | Triangle with a pin | Hatched wall / solid block |

**The key idea:** a pin carries force but no moment, so the member can rotate freely at that point. A fixed support also carries a moment, so the member's end stays at its original angle.

### Effects on structural behaviour

- **Bending moments:** Take a beam under uniform load w and span L. With both ends pinned, the maximum moment is wL²/8 at midspan. With both ends fixed, it is wL²/12 at the supports and wL²/24 at midspan. Fixed ends spread the moment more evenly and lower the peak.
- **Deflection:** Fixed ends make members much stiffer. A fixed-fixed beam deflects about one-fifth as much as a pinned-pinned beam.
- **Column buckling:** The effective length factor K is 1.0 for pinned-pinned columns and in theory 0.5 for fixed-fixed (about 0.65 is used in design). Fixed bases raise buckling capacity a lot.
- **Determinacy:** Each fixed support adds a reaction, so the structure is more statically indeterminate. It then becomes sensitive to settlement, temperature changes and creep.
- **Lateral stability:** A portal frame with pinned bases needs moment-resisting beam-column joints or bracing to stay stable sideways. With fixed bases, the columns themselves act as cantilevers against sway.

### When each is used in buildings

**Pinned supports are used when:**
- **Foundations can't resist moment economically**, such as on soft soil or with small pad footings. A moment at the base would need a much larger footing.
- **Steel column bases have a light base plate and few anchor bolts.** This is the default in many steel frames because it is cheaper and more predictable.
- **Lateral loads go through bracing or cores.** In braced frames and buildings with concrete shear walls or cores, the columns only need to carry gravity loads, so pinned bases are enough.
- **Simple connections are wanted**, such as beam-to-column shear tabs in "simple construction." They are quick to build and easy to analyse.
- **Movement must be allowed.** Pins combined with rollers or sliding bearings let a structure expand with temperature, as in bridges, long roofs and expansion joints.
- **Settlement is a concern**, because determinate structures don't develop extra stresses when a support settles.

**Fixed supports are used when:**
- **Lateral stiffness is needed without bracing.** Moment-frame columns fixed at the base resist wind and earthquake loads and keep floor plans open.
- **Members cantilever**, as in balconies, canopies and overhanging slabs. A cantilever needs a fixed end to work at all.
- **The foundation is stiff**, such as rock, a large raft, pile caps or a basement box, so it can actually resist rotation.
- **Concrete is cast monolithically.** Cast-in-place frames, walls built into footings and slabs continuous over supports are close to fixed by nature.
- **Deflection or vibration controls the design**, for example long spans or sensitive floors.
- **Columns are tall or slender** and need the lower effective length.

### Practical note
Real connections are rarely perfectly pinned or perfectly fixed. A "pinned" base plate still resists some moment, and a "fixed" base on soil still rotates a little. Engineers usually pick the idealisation that is conservative for the thing being checked. For example, they assume pinned when sizing the column for sway, and fixed when designing the footing for moment. When it matters, they model the connection as **semi-rigid** using rotational springs, and codes such as Eurocode 3 and AISC give methods for classifying connection stiffness. Whatever you assume in the analysis must then be built into the detailing: a base assumed fixed needs a thick plate, heavy anchors and a footing able to resist the moment.
