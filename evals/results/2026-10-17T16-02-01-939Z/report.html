const bottomChord: number[] = [];

## Pinned vs. fixed supports

The difference comes down to whether the support can resist **rotation**.

### Pinned support (hinged)
- **Restrains:** translation in every direction. The point can't move.
- **Allows:** rotation. The member can swing freely about the pin.
- **Reactions:** forces only. In 2D that's a horizontal force (Rx) and a vertical force (Ry). In 3D it's Rx, Ry and Rz.
- **Moment at the support:** zero.
- **Symbol:** a triangle sitting on the ground, usually with a small circle (the hinge) at its tip.

### Fixed support (rigid or encastré)
- **Restrains:** all translations and all rotations.
- **Reactions:** forces plus a moment. In 2D that's Rx, Ry and M. In 3D it's three forces and three moments.
- **Moment at the support:** usually the largest moment in the member.
- **Symbol:** a solid wall or ground line with hatching, with the member running straight into it.

### Effect on behaviour

| Aspect | Pinned | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Degrees of freedom restrained (2D) | 2 | 3 |
| Base moment | 0 | Non-zero, often the critical value |
| Frame stiffness and drift | More flexible, larger lateral drift | Stiffer, smaller drift |
| Effective length factor K (column fixed at top) | Higher (column buckles more easily) | Lower (higher buckling capacity) |
| Foundation demand | Vertical and shear forces only, so smaller footings | Must also resist overturning moment, so larger footings, piles or pile caps |
| Connection cost | Simpler and cheaper | Heavier detailing and more expensive |
| Sensitivity to foundation settlement or rotation | Low | High, because the fixity assumption depends on the foundation being rigid |

**Example:** a cantilever of length L with a point load P at its tip has a fixed-end moment of **PL**. A single pin can't support a cantilever at all, because nothing resists rotation, so the structure becomes a mechanism. For a portal frame under lateral load, fixed bases give roughly **2 to 4 times** the lateral stiffness of pinned bases.

### When each is used in buildings

**Pinned supports are typically used for:**
- **Steel column bases in braced frames.** Lateral load goes to bracing or cores, so the base only carries axial load and shear. A plate with 4 anchor bolts is normally modelled as pinned.
- **Simple beam-to-column connections**, such as shear tabs, fin plates and web cleats. These are modelled as pinned so that beams act as simply supported.
- **Sites with weak or compressible soil**, where the foundation can't reliably resist moment.
- **Truss members and roof trusses**, because members are idealised as pin-jointed.
- **Portal frames on poor ground or with modest spans**, where pinned bases keep the foundations small. The frame then has to get its stiffness from rigid knee and apex joints.

**Fixed supports are typically used for:**
- **Moment-resisting frames** with no bracing or cores, where the bases help resist wind and seismic loads and limit drift.
- **Cast-in-place reinforced concrete columns on pad footings, pile caps or rafts.** Monolithic construction naturally gives moment continuity.
- **Cantilevers** such as balconies, canopies, cantilevered stairs and retaining walls. These need a fixed end to work at all.
- **Tall or slender columns**, where fixity cuts the effective length and the buckling risk.
- **Shear walls and cores**, which are fixed at the foundation to resist overturning.
- **Industrial portal frames carrying crane loads**, where tight drift limits apply.

### Practical notes
- **Real connections are semi-rigid.** No base is perfectly pinned or perfectly fixed. Codes such as Eurocode 3 Part 1-8 and AISC classify connections by stiffness. A common practice is to model nominally pinned bases with about 10% of the column stiffness and nominally fixed bases with less than 100%.
- **Choose the model to match the detail.** If you model a base as fixed, the base plate, anchor bolts and footing must be designed for the moment. If you model it as pinned, check that real partial fixity doesn't overload anything, such as the anchor bolts.
- **Check stability.** A structure needs enough restraint to avoid becoming a mechanism. For example, a single-bay frame with pinned bases needs rigid beam-column joints or bracing.
- **Choosing between them is a trade-off.** Fixed bases mean lighter columns and less drift but more expensive foundations. Pinned bases mean cheaper foundations but heavier frames or added bracing.

| **Pinned** (hinged) | Restrained | Restrained | **Free** | 2 forces (Rx, Ry) |
| **Fixed** (rigid, encastré) | Restrained | Restrained | **Restrained** | 2 forces + 1 **moment** (Rx, Ry, M) |
| *Roller (for comparison)* | Free | Restrained | Free | 1 force |

The only difference is **rotation**:
- A **pinned** support holds the member in place but lets it rotate, like a door hinge. It can't resist bending moment, so the moment at a pin is zero.
- A **fixed** support locks position and rotation. It resists bending moment, so the member's end stays at its original angle.

## How this affects the structure

**Bending moments and deflection**
- In a portal frame, fixed column bases spread moment more evenly along the column. You get a moment at the base, smaller peak moments elsewhere, and less sway.
- With pinned bases the base moment is zero. The beams and the column tops carry more moment, and the frame sways more under lateral load such as wind or earthquakes.
- For a beam under uniform load w over span L:
  - Simply supported (pin + roller): max moment = wL²/8, mid-span deflection = 5wL⁴/384EI
  - Fixed at both ends: end moment = wL²/12, mid-span moment = wL²/24, deflection = wL⁴/384EI, which is **5× stiffer**

**Determinacy**
- Fixed supports add reactions, which makes a structure more statically indeterminate. That gives it more redundancy and stiffness, but the analysis is harder, and settlement or temperature changes can cause extra stresses.
- Pinned supports keep a structure simpler and less sensitive to foundation movement.

## When each is used in buildings

### Pinned supports

- **Steel portal frames and sheds** (warehouses, factories): pinned column bases are common. They need smaller foundations because no moment goes into the footing, use simple, cheap base plates (often 2 or 4 bolts), and are easy to put up.
- **Simple beam-to-column connections** in steel frames: shear-only connections such as fin plates and web cleats are designed as pins. Stability then comes from bracing or cores.
- **Sites with poor or variable soil**: pins avoid putting moment into foundations that might rotate or settle.
- **Trusses**: joints are usually idealised as pins so members carry only axial force.
- **Expansion and movement points**, often pin + roller combinations, so thermal movement doesn't build up stress.

### Fixed supports

- **Cast-in-place reinforced concrete frames**: monolithic columns, beams and footings behave close to fixed.
- **Moment-resisting frames** that resist lateral loads without bracing. Fixed bases cut sway (drift), which matters in tall buildings and seismic zones.
- **Cantilevers** such as balconies, canopies, retaining walls and signboards. They *must* be fixed at the root, or they would be mechanisms and collapse.
- **Tall columns or crane buildings** where stiffness and limiting deflection govern the design.
- **Good ground or pile caps** that can resist the base moment without large rotation.

## Practical caveat

Real connections are neither perfectly pinned nor perfectly fixed. They are **semi-rigid**. A "pinned" steel base plate still resists some moment, and a "fixed" base on soft soil still rotates a little. Engineers pick the idealisation that fits the detailing, and sometimes check both cases (for example, assume pinned for foundation design and partial fixity for checking deflection). The key is that **the connection detail must match the assumption in the analysis**. A base designed as fixed needs a thick plate, many anchor bolts and a large footing. A base designed as pinned shouldn't be so stiff that it attracts moment it wasn't designed for.

**In short:** pinned supports allow rotation and carry no moment, so they are simpler and cheaper and suit braced or lightweight structures. Fixed supports prevent rotation and carry moment, so they are stiffer and needed for cantilevers, unbraced frames and drift-sensitive buildings.
